Market Analysis

Stevens County's effective property tax rate of 1.88% is 22.4% above the Kansas state average and 105.2% above the U.S. national average. At this rate, a homeowner with a $400,000 property would pay approximately $7,520 per year in property taxes.

📝 Property Tax Notes

Stevens County presents a notably elevated property tax environment relative to both state and national benchmarks. The county's effective tax rate of 1.88% exceeds the national average of 0.92% by 104%, and surpasses the reported Kansas state average by a full 1.88 percentage points. On a median home value of $129,300, this translates to an annual tax obligation of $2,433—approximately 49% higher than the national median of $1,630. This positions Stevens County firmly as a high-tax jurisdiction within a state context that otherwise reflects moderate property tax burdens. The affordability impact is significant: property taxes consume 3.79% of median household income ($64,178), a threshold that approaches common affordability stress indicators. Over a standard 30-year ownership period, cumulative property tax payments on a median-priced home reach $72,990—equivalent to 56% of the home's current median value. This long-term cost projection underscores that property taxes represent a substantial recurring expense that rivals mortgage principal in total outlay. Prospective buyers should factor this elevated carrying cost into purchase calculus, recognizing that the tax burden effectively reduces purchasing power by approximately $40,000 to $50,000 in mortgage capacity compared to national-average jurisdictions. Current owners should verify assessment accuracy annually, as Kansas law permits appeals through the County Board of Equalization. Homestead and senior exemptions may provide relief for qualifying residents, while agricultural classifications could affect land-heavy parcels. A concrete insight: despite the high effective rate, the relatively low median home value means absolute tax dollars remain moderate compared to high-value coastal markets; however, the income-adjusted burden is severe, creating a regressive dynamic where lower-valued homes in lower-income areas bear disproportionate tax weight relative to earning capacity.
