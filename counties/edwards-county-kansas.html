Market Analysis

Edwards County's effective property tax rate of 1.58% is 2.9% above the Kansas state average and 72.5% above the U.S. national average. At this rate, a homeowner with a $400,000 property would pay approximately $6,320 per year in property taxes.

📝 Property Tax Notes

The effective property‑tax rate in Edwards County stands at 1.58 %, which is 0.66 percentage points above the national average of 0.92 % and 1.58 points higher than the Kansas state average (reported as 0.00 %). This places the county firmly in the high‑tax tier relative to both state and national benchmarks; the median annual levy of $1,120 exceeds the national median tax of $1,630 only in dollar terms because the local home‑value base is substantially lower ($70,900 versus the national median). When expressed as a share of median household income ($52,692), the property‑tax burden equals 2.13 % of earnings. Compared with the national average property‑tax‑to‑income ratio of roughly 3 %, Edwards County’s share is modest, suggesting that despite a higher rate, the low home values keep the absolute burden relatively affordable. A 2.13 % allocation is generally sustainable for median‑income families, leaving ample margin for other housing costs such as mortgage principal, insurance, and utilities. Over a 30‑year horizon, a homeowner of a median‑priced residence would pay $33,600 in property taxes ($1,120 × 30). This cumulative amount represents about 1.9 % of the total nominal value of the home ($70,900 × 30 ≈ $2.13 million in assessed value over time), illustrating that long‑term tax exposure remains proportionally low despite the elevated rate. Prospective buyers should factor the $1,120 annual levy into cash‑flow calculations, verify any local assessment cycles, and consider the potential for lower‑rate exemptions that many Kansas counties offer for seniors, veterans, or agricultural landowners. Current owners can explore homestead exemptions, senior‑citizen credits, or agricultural use assessments, which often reduce the effective rate by 0.2–0.5 percentage points. A less obvious insight is that the county’s high effective rate is largely a function of a low valuation base rather than an aggressive levy; if property values were to rise toward the national median, the absolute tax bill would increase substantially even if the rate remained unchanged.
