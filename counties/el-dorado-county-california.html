📝 Property Tax Notes

The effective property‑tax rate in El Dorado County (0.70 %) sits 0.22 percentage points below the national average of 0.92 % and 0.70 percentage points above the state‑wide figure reported as 0.00 % (reflecting California’s generally low nominal rates after Proposition 13 caps). Relative to other U.S. jurisdictions, El Dorado is a low‑tax environment; the median annual levy of $4,753 is roughly three times the national median tax of $1,630, but this difference is driven by the county’s substantially higher median home value ($679,900 versus the national median) rather than a higher rate. Consequently, the tax burden is modest when expressed as a share of income. Property‑tax payments consume 4.37 % of the median household income ($108,845), a proportion that aligns with the national average of 4‑5 % for owner‑occupied housing. At this level, the expense is generally sustainable for median‑income families, leaving ample margin for mortgage principal, insurance, and other cost of living items. The 30‑year cumulative tax outlay on a median‑priced home totals $142,590, derived from the 5‑year total ($23,765) multiplied by six, assuming static rates and values. This long‑term figure underscores that, while annual payments are moderate, the aggregate cost over a typical home‑ownership horizon is significant and should be incorporated into total‑cost‑of‑ownership calculations. Prospective buyers should note that the relatively low rate does not translate into low dollar amounts because of high property values; budgeting for an annual $4,753 tax bill is essential, especially when combined with mortgage interest and maintenance costs. Current owners may benefit from California’s Proposition 13 protections, which limit annual assessment increases to 2 % plus inflation, and from statewide exemptions such as the Homeowners’ Exemption ($7,000 reduction in assessed value) and the senior/disabled homeowner exemptions, which can further reduce the effective rate. A less obvious insight is that the county’s tax advantage stems more from the capped assessment methodology than from a deliberately low levy, meaning that future reassessments after a change of ownership could produce a step‑up in taxable value, markedly raising the annual tax burden for new owners.
