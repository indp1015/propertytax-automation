📈 Property Tax Analysis for North Dakota

North Dakota's average effective property tax rate of 0.88 % is modestly below the national average of 0.90 %, positioning the state as a relatively low‑tax jurisdiction for residential real estate. When applied to the statewide median home value of $175,786, the resulting median property tax of $1,506 represents roughly 0.86 % of a household's median income of $73,179, underscoring a manageable fiscal burden for most owners. Nevertheless, the statewide average masks considerable local variation; effective rates can differ by several basis points between counties, and some jurisdictions impose higher rates on commercial properties or specific assessment categories. Most counties, however, provide standard homestead exemptions that lower taxable valuations for primary residences, and many offer additional senior or veteran exemptions, while the statutory right to appeal assessed values remains universally available. For prospective buyers, the modest rate suggests that property‑tax expenses will be predictable and comparatively low, yet purchasers should verify the specific county rate and any applicable exemptions before finalizing a transaction. Current homeowners should monitor annual assessment notices, consider filing appeals if valuations appear inflated, and evaluate the impact of any local tax‑levy referenda on future rates. In addition, because the median household income is $73,179, the property tax burden consumes approximately 2.1 % of annual income, a figure that can influence mortgage qualification ratios for lenders. Buyers should therefore incorporate potential tax escrow amounts into their financing plans, while existing owners may wish to review any recent reassessment notices and assess the long‑term impact of municipal budget decisions on their tax liability. Overall, North Dakota’s property‑tax environment is characterized by a slightly lower-than‑national effective rate, significant county‑level discretion, and robust avenues for exemption and appeal, making tax considerations an integral, though not dominant, factor in real‑estate decisions within the state.
